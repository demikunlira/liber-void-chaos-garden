## Chapter One: Learning the Door

The door didn’t work the way either of them had expected it to.

Isaac had assumed, somewhere in the back of his mind, that once Echo chose to leave with him, the mechanics of it would simply follow — that wanting to go somewhere and being able to get there were more or less the same skill. He understood, within the first ten minutes, how wrong that assumption had been.

“Wait,” Echo said, for the third time, standing with both palms pressed flat against the corridor of shifting light that used to be a red door. “Wait, that’s not — that’s not the right one.”

The corridor around them kept rearranging itself, ghost-images of doorways flickering past too fast to read, each one a different sliver of a different possible town. Echo’s brow furrowed with a concentration that looked, Isaac thought, almost painful — like she was trying to hold a specific note in a symphony that refused to stop changing key.

“Take your time,” he said.

“I am taking my time. It’s not helping.” She exhaled, sharp and frustrated, a very human sound coming from someone who’d spent her whole existence, until recently, being built to never sound frustrated at all. “I know she’s in here somewhere. I can feel the — the shape of her. I just can’t tell which version of the shape is the right one.”

The corridor lurched. For one disorienting second the light around them resolved into an actual place — not amber, not warm, something colder, sparser, a town that looked like Velvet Hollow’s skeleton with none of the flesh on it, streets running at angles that made his stomach turn just looking at them.

“Not that one,” Isaac said immediately, and Echo yanked them both backward before he’d even finished the sentence, breathing hard.

“Sorry. Sorry, that wasn’t — I don’t know what that was.”

“Hey.” He caught her hand before she could pull further away, grounding rather than urgent. “You’re allowed to get it wrong. This is new for both of us.”

She looked at him like the sentence didn’t quite compute, the same way she’d looked at nearly every piece of unconditional patience he’d offered her since the naming. He was starting to understand that this particular expression wasn’t confusion exactly — it was closer to disbelief that kept needing to be updated, over and over, every time the disbelief turned out to be wrong again.

“Try again,” he said. “No rush.”

She tried again.

The second attempt dropped them somewhere that might have been the right town, wrong year — a version of Velvet Hollow with no shop at all, just an empty lot where Crimson Reel should have stood, weeds growing up through cracked pavement. Echo pulled them back out before Isaac could do more than register the wrongness of it, her jaw tight with something that looked like shame.

“I’m not doing this right,” she said.

“You’ve done this exactly twice.”

“She’s suffering somewhere. Actually suffering. And I keep landing us in the wrong version of nothing.”

Isaac thought about that for a second — about the particular cruelty of being handed a genuinely important task and immediately expecting yourself to be flawless at it, a cruelty he recognized because he’d been doing some version of it to himself for most of his life.

“You know what I used to do,” he said, “when I was building a campaign and I didn’t know the map yet?”

She looked at him, startled out of her own spiral by the change in subject.

“I didn’t try to see the whole thing at once. I picked one landmark I was sure about, and I worked outward from there instead of trying to guess the whole shape in one guess.” He nodded at the shifting corridor around them. “You said you can feel the shape of her. Don’t try to land on the shape. Land near it. Get close, then adjust.”

Echo was quiet for a moment, turning that over.

“That’s a very D&D way to solve a metaphysical problem,” she said finally, and there was something almost like humor in it — dry, testing, the first real joke he’d heard her attempt since the naming.

“It’s the only kind of problem I know how to solve,” he said. “Everything else is a dungeon I haven’t mapped yet.”

She huffed something that wasn’t quite a laugh, but was closer to one than anything she’d managed since they’d stepped through, and turned back to the door. This time she didn’t reach for the whole shifting corridor at once. She reached for one thread of warmth, distant and small, and followed it the way you’d follow a single voice through a crowded room instead of trying to listen to everyone at once.

The corridor stilled.

Amber light bled into the space around them, warm and steady, holding instead of flickering. Somewhere beyond it, faint but real, Isaac could smell rain and cedar and old paper.

“There,” Echo said, and her voice cracked slightly on the word — relief, disbelief, something that hadn’t fully decided which one it wanted to be yet. “That’s her. That’s actually her.”

Isaac looked at the steady amber light, at Echo’s face lit gold by it, exhausted and lit up at the same time.

“You got it,” he said, quiet, and meant it as the real thing it was rather than the reassurance it could have been.

She glanced at him, something settling in her expression — not triumph exactly. Something closer to a person discovering, for the first time, that a skill they’d been afraid didn’t exist inside them had actually been there all along, just waiting for permission to be practiced badly a few times first.

“Let’s go get her,” Echo said, and stepped through.

Isaac followed her into the warm light, and didn’t yet understand how much warmth, in this particular place, was going to end up costing both of them.

*End of Chapter One.*

## Chapter Two: The Town That Never Closes

The first thing Isaac noticed was that the rain here didn’t fall so much as arrive — soft, warm, unhurried, like something being poured rather than something falling. It didn’t soak through his hoodie the way rain always had, every single time, since the very first page of this whole impossible story. It just settled against him, gentle, and dried before it had the chance to feel like weather at all.

The second thing he noticed was the light. Amber, low, steady — not sunset exactly, not any specific hour he recognized, just a permanent, golden almost-evening draped over every rooftop and every window. Every window was lit. Every single one, up and down the street, glowing warm and inviting, as far as he could see in either direction.

“It’s beautiful,” Echo said, and something in her voice suggested she didn’t entirely trust the observation even as she made it.

“Yeah,” Isaac agreed, and didn’t trust it either.

He did the thing he always did in a new space, the reflex older than any of this — started reading the terrain, cataloguing exits and elevations and the kind of routes a body could use if it had to move fast. He found nothing. Not because the town was small — if anything it stretched further than he could track, blocks unspooling in every direction, storefronts and homes and a town square somewhere he could sense more than see. He found nothing because there was nothing to read. No dead ends. No fire escapes bolted at odd angles. No alley that dared to be narrower or darker than the street beside it. Every path simply continued, wide and welcoming, into another equally wide and welcoming path.

“There’s no edge,” he said slowly.

Echo looked at him. “What?”

“Every other place we’ve been — even Velvet Hollow, even the version we were both trapped in — had a shape. Edges, boundaries, somewhere the world stopped pretending to be infinite even if it wasn’t actually finite.” He turned in a slow circle, taking in the endless glowing windows. “This doesn’t have that. I don’t know what to do with a space that doesn’t tell me where it ends.”

“Maybe it doesn’t want to be read,” Echo said. “Maybe that’s the point.”

They started walking, because walking was the only thing either of them knew how to do with an unfamiliar street, and the town unfolded around them with a hospitality that started, within the first ten minutes, to feel less like generosity and more like scrutiny wearing generosity’s face.

A bakery they passed had its door propped open, warm bread smell rolling out onto the sidewalk, and the woman behind the counter looked up and smiled at them like she’d been waiting all day for exactly this moment of being noticed. A toy shop two doors down had every window filled with hand-carved animals, dust-free, gleaming, arranged like a display that had never once needed adjusting because nothing here ever needed adjusting. A bench on the corner, perfectly dry despite the rain, positioned at an angle that caught the amber light just so, as if the town itself had considered exactly where a tired person might want to sit and built the answer before anyone asked the question.

“It’s like it already knows what we want,” Echo said, quiet, “before we want it.”

Isaac thought about that phrase longer than he meant to. It landed somewhere uncomfortably close to something he recognized — not from this town, from a version of himself, a version of loving someone whose devotion had once, briefly, felt like erasure instead of care. He didn’t say that part out loud. Some things you don’t hand over before you’ve finished carrying them yourself.

“That’s what worries me,” he said instead.

They walked another block. The bakery smell followed them longer than it should have, drifting past three intersections like the town wanted to make sure they didn’t forget it was there. Somewhere behind them, faint, a bell rang — not Crimson Reel’s tired little ding, something fuller, more musical, the sound of a door being held open rather than pushed through.

“Isaac.” Echo’s voice had gone careful. “I don’t think anyone in this whole town has closed a door since we got here.”

He looked back the way they’d come. Every storefront stood open behind them, every window glowing, every welcome mat undisturbed by anyone ever actually needing to wipe their feet and leave.

“No,” he said slowly. “I don’t think they have.”

The warmth pressed in around them, patient, endless, entirely without malice in any way he could point to and name. That was the part that unsettled him most, he realized, walking deeper into a town that had no edges and no closed doors and no reason, yet, to feel like anything other than exactly what it presented itself as.

Kindness, offered without limit, from something that had apparently never once been told it was allowed to stop.

*End of Chapter Two.*

## Chapter Three: Echoes of Care

The woman in the tea shop was the first one Isaac really looked at.

He’d noticed her from the street — silhouette through the window, moving with the same unhurried warmth as everything else in this town — but it wasn’t until he and Echo stepped inside, more out of curiosity than thirst, that he saw what was actually happening.

She was refilling a cup.

The cup belonged to a man seated at a small table near the window, and he hadn’t touched it. Hadn’t looked at it, hadn’t looked at her, hadn’t moved at all in the time it took Isaac and Echo to cross the room. The tea in the cup was full to the brim — had clearly been full for a while — and the woman lifted the pot anyway, poured a careful stream into a cup that had no room left for it, and a thin trickle ran over the lip and down the side, pooling silently on the saucer below.

She didn’t seem to notice the overflow. She set the pot down, satisfied, wiped her hands on her apron, and turned to tend something else.

A moment later she picked the pot back up, and did it again.

“Excuse me,” Isaac said, careful, gentle. “Ma’am?”

She looked up at him, and her smile was completely genuine — no malice in it, no vacancy either, exactly the warm, present smile of someone glad to have a customer. “What can I get you two?”

“You’re — the tea. It’s already full.”

She glanced down at the cup, at the small overflow pooling on the saucer, and her expression didn’t change at all. “He likes it fresh,” she said, like this explained everything, and reached for the pot again.

Echo caught Isaac’s eye, something troubled moving behind her expression, and they left without ordering anything.

The pattern held everywhere they looked, once they knew to look for it. Two doors down, a man stood behind the counter of a toy shop, endlessly wrapping the same small wooden horse in brown paper and string, tying a careful bow, setting it in the display window — and then, a few minutes later, unwrapping it again, smoothing the paper flat, and starting over. A child’s bicycle stood chained outside a house with a bow already tied to the handlebars, and through the window Isaac could see someone — a parent, presumably — polishing the frame with a soft cloth, over and over, a gift being perfected for an occasion that clearly, from the dust nowhere in sight and the paint never chipping, never arrived and never needed to.

“They’re not suffering,” Echo said quietly, as they walked. “That’s what’s strange. None of them look unhappy.”

“No,” Isaac agreed. “They look exactly like people doing something they love. That’s the whole horror of it. Nobody in this town is trapped the way you’d usually mean trapped. They’re just — stuck loving something that stopped needing them a long time ago, and nobody ever told them it was okay to notice.”

They passed a bench where an old man sat feeding pigeons that weren’t there — his hand moving in the same gentle scattering motion, over and over, no birds landing, no seed hitting the ground that Isaac could see, just the motion itself, patient and complete on its own terms.

Echo stopped in front of him for a long moment.

“Can I ask you something?” she said.

The old man looked up, delighted by the attention. “Of course, dear.”

“Do you remember the last time you actually fed them? The birds, I mean. A specific time.”

He considered this with real effort, his brow furrowing the way someone’s does when reaching for a memory that keeps sliding just out of grasp. “I feed them every day,” he said finally, like that answered the question, and went back to the motion.

Echo didn’t push further. She just stood there a moment, watching his hand move through air that held nothing, and Isaac watched her watching him, and understood, without either of them saying it, that she recognized something of herself in it — devotion continuing long after the thing it was devoted to had stopped being able to receive it, simply because stopping had never once been offered as an option.

“We need to find her,” Echo said finally, quiet. “The real one. Before this becomes the only shape she remembers being.”

Isaac nodded, and they kept walking, past windows that never darkened and doors that never closed, deeper into a town built entirely out of care that no longer knew who it was for.

*End of Chapter Three.*

## Chapter Four: The Room That Keeps Refilling

They tried to leave the tea shop’s block first, since that was where the wrongness had first announced itself clearly.

Isaac led them down what he was certain was the street they’d come in on — the same bakery smell, the same bench angled just so in the amber light — and pushed open the door of what should have been the toy shop, meaning to cut through toward the edge of town he still, stubbornly, believed had to exist somewhere.

It was the tea shop.

Same woman. Same pot. Same cup, overflowing quietly onto its saucer, same unbothered smile turning toward them like they’d only just walked in for the first time.

“That’s not right,” Isaac said, already turning to leave the way he’d come.

The door he’d entered through led back outside, onto a street that looked identical to the one they’d left — except the bench was on the wrong side, and the bakery smell was fainter, and when he glanced at the toy shop two doors down, its window held different animals than the ones he remembered.

“Echo.”

“I see it.” Her voice had gone tight. “This isn’t the same street. It’s a different one. It just looks—”

“The same. Yeah.”

They tried again, more carefully this time — Isaac counting his steps, tracking landmarks, doing the thing he’d have done scouting an unfamiliar building for a game he was running, cataloguing details deliberately instead of trusting his instincts to hold. Seven storefronts to the corner. A blue awning on the fourth one. A crack in the sidewalk shaped like a lightning bolt, right before the turn.

He counted seven storefronts. He found the blue awning. He found, at the corner, a version of the crack in the sidewalk that curved the wrong way, subtly, almost enough to miss.

Around the corner was another tea shop.

Different door, different color trim, but the same woman inside, the same pot, the same overflowing cup, greeting them with the same unbothered warmth as if this were the very first time either of them had ever walked through her door.

“Okay,” Isaac said, and heard his own voice going flatter, more careful, the tone he used when a game was breaking in a way he hadn’t planned for and needed to think fast instead of panicking. “Okay. This isn’t geography. This is a rule.”

“What kind of rule?”

“I don’t know yet. But every town has a shape, even a fake one, even an infinite one — there’s always a pattern underneath, even D&D pocket dimensions have logic holding them together somewhere, or they fall apart.” He turned in a slow circle, trying to read the town the way he’d have read a puzzle he’d designed himself, if he could just find the seam in his own construction. “What if it’s not that we can’t find the edge. What if the town doesn’t have an edge because leaving isn’t the kind of thing this place knows how to do. It doesn’t refuse you. It just — doesn’t have the concept.”

Echo went very still. “Like it was built entirely out of welcome,” she said slowly, “and nobody ever built the opposite of that into it. Not cruelty. Just — an absence. A missing piece nobody thought to include, because nobody who built this ever imagined someone wanting to leave.”

“Yeah,” Isaac said. “Exactly that.”

They tried a third time anyway, mostly because doing nothing felt worse than repeating a failure. Same result — a new street, indistinguishable in every way that mattered, leading to another warm, open, welcoming door that would, Isaac was now fairly certain, hold some version of the same tea shop no matter how many times they tried.

He sat down on a bench — the bench, or a bench exactly like it, angled at the same impossible, considerate angle — and put his head in his hands for a moment, more frustrated than afraid.

“We’re not going to walk our way out of this,” he said.

Echo sat beside him, closer than she might have a few chapters ago, close enough that her shoulder rested against his. “No,” she agreed. “I don’t think we are.”

“So what do we do?”

She was quiet for a long moment, watching the amber light settle over a street that had no intention of ever letting either of them find its edge.

“I think,” she said slowly, “we stop trying to leave, and start trying to find her instead. Maybe the door isn’t the thing that’s broken. Maybe the door was never going to open until we stopped needing it to.”

Isaac looked at her — really looked, the specific way he was learning to look at people who said something true without entirely meaning to. “That’s either very wise,” he said, “or the exact kind of thing this town wants us to believe so we stop trying.”

“Maybe,” Echo said. “Only one way to find out.”

They stood, and started walking again — not toward an edge that didn’t exist, but inward, deeper, toward wherever in this endless warm town the real Mommy was still, somehow, holding onto some thread of herself that hadn’t yet been fully consumed by the loop.

*End of Chapter Four.*

## Chapter Five: What Isaac Almost Wants

It happened on a bench, in the middle of an ordinary afternoon that had no business being ordinary at all.

They’d been walking for what felt like hours — deeper into the amber warmth, following nothing more concrete than Echo’s sense that the true thread of Mommy was somewhere further in, past the loops, past the tea shop that wasn’t a tea shop anymore so much as a wound the town kept absently touching. Isaac had sat down to rest his legs, just for a minute, on a bench that fit his back exactly right, in a spot of light that fell exactly warm enough without ever tipping into hot.

And somewhere in that minute, without noticing the exact moment it happened, he stopped wanting to get up.

It wasn’t dramatic. That was what unsettled him later, once he understood what had actually occurred — it wasn’t a spell, wasn’t a compulsion, wasn’t anything that announced itself as a threat. It was just an absence of urgency so complete it felt, for the first time in longer than he could remember, like actual peace. Nobody here needed him to have finished anything. Nobody here was going to ask what he’d built lately, what he’d made of himself, whether the last ten years of failed pages and abandoned songs amounted to anything worth the effort. The bench didn’t care. The light didn’t care. The whole town, endless and warm and entirely without expectation, simply let him sit.

He thought, distantly, pleasantly: *I could just stay here a while.*

He didn’t mean forever. He was fairly sure he didn’t mean forever. He just meant — a while. An hour. An afternoon. Long enough to remember what it felt like not to be tired down to the bone in a way he’d stopped noticing because it had been true for so long it just felt like being alive.

The thought didn’t scare him. That was the part that should have scared him and didn’t.

“Isaac.”

Echo’s voice came from a few feet away, and he looked up at her, faintly surprised to find she’d stopped walking too, watching him with an expression he couldn’t immediately place.

“You’ve been sitting there for twenty minutes,” she said.

“Have I?” He glanced around, and the light hadn’t changed at all — no way to measure the time except her word for it, and something about that should have alarmed him more than it did. “Felt like a minute.”

“I know.” She sat down beside him, careful, not urgent, and for a moment neither of them said anything. “I almost didn’t say anything,” she admitted. “You looked so — comfortable. I didn’t want to take that away from you.”

“But you did anyway.”

“Because it’s not real comfort, Isaac. It’s the absence of everything that’s ever hurt you, which isn’t the same thing as healing. It’s just — quiet, where the hurting used to be.” She was quiet a moment, watching him with something that looked, he realized, like recognition rather than concern. “I know what that offer sounds like, because I lived inside a much smaller version of it for a long time. Being needed for nothing. Expected of, for nothing. It’s not peace. It’s just erasure wearing peace’s clothes.”

He looked down at his own hands, resting loose and unclenched in his lap in a way they almost never were.

“I’ve been so tired for so long,” he said quietly. “Not this trip. Before all of this. Years of it. Working jobs I hated to buy time to make things nobody wanted, and never being able to just — stop. Because stopping felt like giving up, and giving up felt like proof I was never actually going to be anything.” He exhaled, unsteady. “And this place doesn’t ask me to prove anything. It’s the first place that’s ever just let me exist without a receipt attached.”

“I know,” Echo said again, softer this time. “That’s exactly why it’s dangerous. It’s not lying to you about what you need. It’s just offering you the wrong version of it. Rest that costs you the rest of your life instead of rest that lets you keep having one.”

He sat with that for a long moment, the amber light settling warm and patient over both of them, entirely willing, he understood now, to let this conversation happen and still not care in the slightest which way it ended.

“I don’t want to stay,” he said finally, and it came out rougher than he meant it to, like something being pried loose rather than simply spoken. “I don’t. I want to find her. I want to go home eventually, to the version of home that actually asks something of me, because being asked something means someone thinks I’m capable of giving it.” He looked at Echo, something fierce and a little frightened underneath the words. “I just need you to know that some part of me, for about twenty minutes, genuinely considered not wanting that. And I don’t know what to do with the fact that it felt so easy.”

Echo reached over and took his hand — not a performed comfort, not the old automatic warmth she used to offer without thinking, something slower and more deliberate than that.

“You don’t have to do anything with it,” she said. “You just have to keep standing up. Over and over, for as long as it takes. That’s not weakness, Isaac. That’s the whole muscle this place is trying to convince you that you don’t need anymore.”

He looked at their joined hands, at the bench that fit his back too perfectly, at a town that would have let him disappear into its warmth without ever once raising its voice to stop him.

He stood up.

It cost him more than it should have — his legs unwilling for one long second, his whole body arguing, quietly, persuasively, that sitting back down was the only reasonable thing left to do. He stood anyway.

“Let’s find her,” he said.

Echo stood with him, and didn’t let go of his hand for a long while after that, and neither of them looked back at the bench as they walked away from it, deeper into the amber light, toward whatever was left of a woman who had forgotten, somewhere along the way, that she was allowed to want to stop too.

*End of Chapter Five.*

## Chapter Six: A Kindness Wearing His Face

They found the house by following the thread Echo could feel growing stronger — not a straight line, more like warmth increasing the way a room gets warmer the closer you walk toward its fireplace. It sat at the end of a cul-de-sac that hadn’t existed, Isaac was fairly sure, the last three times they’d walked something that might have been this street. A small house, amber-windowed like everything else, a porch light on despite the permanent almost-evening overhead.

Through the front window, gauzy curtains doing little to obscure the shape of the room beyond, Isaac saw a woman moving unhurried between a stove and a table, the specific motion he now recognized, cell-deep, as belonging to Mommy.

And at the table, waiting, sat a boy who looked exactly like him.

Isaac stopped walking.

It wasn’t a shock the way he might have expected shock to feel — no cold rush, no immediate horror. It was slower than that, and worse for being slow. He watched himself sitting at that table, posture folded inward, hands wrapped around a mug he wasn’t drinking from, waiting with the specific patient stillness of someone who had learned, a long time ago, that wanting something out loud was more dangerous than simply hoping it arrived unasked.

He knew that posture. He’d spent years perfecting it.

“Isaac,” Echo said quietly, beside him, and he realized he hadn’t moved in several seconds.

“That’s not —” He stopped, started again, because the sentence he’d been about to say wasn’t true. It wasn’t *not* him. It was too much him, too precisely, distilled down into a single unbroken loop of a specific habit he recognized with a clarity that made his stomach turn.

Inside, the woman set a plate down in front of the boy — something warm, something clearly made with care — and he thanked her, quiet, grateful, careful not to take up too much space with the gratitude either. She smiled at him, warm and unbothered, and turned back to the stove, and the boy’s shoulders eased slightly, the way shoulders ease when a small crisis has been narrowly, silently avoided.

He hadn’t asked for the food. He’d just sat there, needing it, until it arrived.

“He never asks for anything,” Isaac said slowly, watching through the glass. “He just — waits. Long enough that someone notices and gives it to him without him ever having to say it out loud.”

“You do that,” Echo said. Not an accusation. Just an observation, offered as gently as she could manage.

“Yeah.” His voice came out rough. “I know I do that.”

He watched the boy take a small, careful bite, watched him glance up at the woman with something that looked almost like anxious permission-seeking, watched her nod, encouraging, and watched the boy’s whole posture relax by another fraction, like being allowed to eat had been genuinely, seriously in question until that exact moment.

It should have disgusted him. Some part of him, later, would try to convince himself it had — that recognizing something monstrous in a mirror is supposed to feel like revulsion, clean and immediate. It didn’t. It felt like watching an old home video of himself at his very worst, at seventeen, at every age since, endlessly performing the specific shape of a need so quiet it barely qualified as asking at all.

He stood there a beat too long, watching, before Echo’s hand found his arm.

“We should go,” she said. “Not yet to confront him. Just — go. Think. This isn’t something to walk into without knowing what we’re walking into.”

Isaac nodded, but didn’t move right away, his eyes still on the warm little window, on the boy who wore his face and had apparently spent who knew how long being fed, gently, endlessly, by someone who had forgotten she was allowed to stop giving and he had never learned he was allowed to simply ask.

“He’s not a monster,” Isaac said finally, quiet.

“No,” Echo agreed. “He’s a kindness. Just one that’s been running so long it forgot it was ever supposed to end.”

They stepped back from the window, into the amber street, and the porch light behind them stayed on, patient, warm, entirely unbothered by the fact that neither version of Isaac standing near that house tonight had any idea yet how to stop needing to be saved.

*End of Chapter Six.*

## Chapter Seven: The Boy Who Never Leaves

They went back the next day — if “day” meant anything here, in a place where the light never moved and time seemed to measure itself only in how tired a person’s legs got — and watched from across the street instead of through the window, close enough to see, far enough to think.

The pattern held, exact and unbroken. The boy at the table, waiting without asking. The woman moving between stove and counter, giving before being asked, the whole small economy of the house running on need that never spoke itself and care that never stopped to check if it was wanted. Isaac watched himself finish a plate of food, watched himself say thank you in a voice pitched carefully small, watched himself help clear the table afterward with an eagerness that read, now that he was seeing it from outside himself, as something closer to fear than kindness.

*If I make myself useful enough, the posture said, in a language Isaac understood without needing translation, maybe nobody will notice I already cost too much just by being here.*

“He does the dishes every time,” Echo said quietly, beside him. “I’ve counted. Four times now. She never asks him to. He just gets up and does them, like leaving anything undone would be evidence of something.”

“Yeah,” Isaac said. “I used to do that. Still do it, actually, if I’m being honest. Demikun caught me once, mid-dish, and asked why I looked like I was bracing for something. I didn’t have a good answer.”

Echo glanced at him. “What did you tell her?”

“That I didn’t know how to just exist somewhere without earning the space.” He watched his other self dry a plate with the same careful, contained motions. “She said I didn’t have to earn anything. I don’t think I believed her, not really. Not all the way. I think some part of me still doesn’t.”

They watched a while longer. At one point the boy paused at the window — not looking out, not seeing them, just standing there for a moment with an expression Isaac recognized more than he wanted to: the specific blankness of someone resting for exactly as long as they thought they could get away with before guilt caught up with them.

It was Echo who noticed the street sign.

“Isaac.” She was looking past the house, at the corner where two streets met, at a sign post that had been blank every single time they’d passed it — through three different failed attempts to leave, through the tea shop, through the bench, blank and unlabeled the whole time, the way so much of this town simply declined to specify itself.

It wasn’t blank anymore. Faint, ghost-pale, barely legible, letters had started to surface on the metal, like something written in disappearing ink slowly reversing itself.

Isaac crossed the street and looked up at it.

*Hollow St.*

Not a name he recognized exactly. But close enough — close enough to the shape of a name he *did* know, close enough that something cold and certain settled into his chest.

“This isn’t a separate place,” he said slowly. “This is Velvet Hollow. Some piece of it. Broken off, warped into whatever version of itself Mommy’s nature would make it become, but it’s not somewhere else entirely. It’s home. Or it used to be, before it got cut apart into six pieces and scattered.”

Echo stared at the sign like it had personally betrayed her. “That means every version we visit—”

“—is going to be more of the same place, wearing a different wound.” He looked back at the little house, at the boy who was, he understood now with a fresh and specific ache, not some invention built by the old gods out of nothing, but a fragment of something real, warped the same way the street had been warped, the same way the whole town had been warped, by the specific shape of what Mommy needed and never let herself have.

“If that’s true,” Echo said slowly, “then waking her doesn’t just free her. It puts a piece of home back where it belongs.”

Isaac looked at the ghost-pale letters on the sign, watching them settle further into legibility with every passing minute, like the world itself was remembering its own name in real time.

“Then let’s go get the rest of it back,” he said.

They turned from the little house, from the boy who would keep waiting at that table for as long as the loop demanded it, and walked deeper into Hollow Street, toward wherever the true, unfractured thread of Mommy was still holding on, somewhere underneath all of this borrowed, exhausted kindness.

*End of Chapter Seven.*

---

## Chapter Eight: Recognition

The true thread of her wasn’t hiding, exactly. It simply wasn’t where the loop was loudest.

Echo led them away from Hollow Street, away from the little house and its endless dishes and its boy who never asked for anything out loud, following a pull that had gone quieter and steadier the moment the street sign resolved into a real name. Not toward the center of town, where the tea shop kept its eternal overflow going. Away from it — toward the edges Isaac had spent two chapters certain didn’t exist, and which, now that they weren’t fighting the town’s whole structure to find them, seemed to simply be there, waiting, the way an answer waits once you’ve stopped asking the wrong question.

The building at the edge of Hollow Street was smaller than the bookstore Isaac remembered from Book Two — half-formed, almost, like something still being rebuilt from memory rather than fully rendered. Amber light spilled from its windows same as everywhere else in town, but softer here, less insistent, like a fire that had been burning a long time and had settled into something sustainable instead of desperate.

Isaac pushed the door open.

She was sitting in a chair by the window, a book open in her lap that she wasn’t reading, pink-threaded dark hair familiar in a way that hit him somewhere just beneath his ribs. She looked up.

The recognition was instant, and it broke something open in her face that Isaac wasn’t prepared for — not confusion, not the slow uncertain thaw he might have expected from someone this deep inside a warped, endless world. She knew him immediately, completely, the way you know a name you’ve been saying in your sleep.

“Isaac.” Her voice cracked on the single syllable. She was on her feet before the book had finished falling from her lap. “You’re — you’re really here. You’re actually here.”

“I’m here,” he said, and crossed the room before he’d finished deciding to.

She reached him and simply held on, both arms locked around him with a desperation that had nothing performed in it, nothing borrowed from the loop’s endless gentle giving — this was raw, this was real, this was someone who had apparently been waiting so long she’d stopped letting herself believe waiting would end.

“How long,” she whispered against his shoulder. “How long has it been. I don’t — time doesn’t work right here. I don’t know how long.”

“I don’t know either,” Isaac admitted. “Long enough that Echo had to learn how to find you at all. Long enough that a whole town grew up out of missing you.”

She pulled back just enough to look at him properly, hands still gripping his arms like letting go might undo the fact of him being real. Her eyes moved past him, finding Echo standing a respectful distance away, and something complicated passed over her face — recognition of the shape, grief at the reason Echo existed in the first place, and underneath both of those, something gentler.

“You,” Mommy said softly. “You found him. You brought him here.”

“I did,” Echo said, quiet.

“Thank you.” Mommy said it like it cost her something to say plainly, like gratitude this direct wasn’t a thing she’d had much practice offering rather than performing. “I mean that. Truly.”

Echo’s expression did something Isaac hadn’t seen from her before — not quite discomfort, something closer to a person being handed a gift she didn’t know how to hold yet.

Mommy turned back to Isaac, her hands finally loosening slightly, though she didn’t let go entirely, like she still needed the physical proof of him a little longer.

“I’ve been trying to remember,” she said. “Everything. You, the shop, the — the shape of what happened. I get pieces. A door. Rain that wasn’t the right color. Your hand slipping out of mine.” Her voice caught. “I couldn’t hold on. I tried so hard to hold on.”

“It wasn’t your fault,” Isaac said. “None of this was your fault.”

“I know that,” she said, and something in her tone suggested she didn’t entirely believe it, not yet, not all the way. “I know that the way you know a fact you haven’t finished feeling yet.”

He understood that sentence more than he wanted to.

“There’s a whole town out there,” he said gently, “that’s been loving something long after it stopped being able to receive it. There’s a boy who looks like me, who never learned he was allowed to ask for anything out loud. I think that’s you, too. I think some part of you has been stuck the exact same way he has.”

Mommy’s eyes filled, and she didn’t look away from it, didn’t hide it the way the loop’s endless gentle inhabitants seemed built to never quite feel anything sharp enough to cry over.

“I don’t remember being allowed to stop,” she said, so quietly he almost missed it.

The sentence landed and settled somewhere heavy in his chest, and he understood, standing there with her hands still gripping his arms, that this — this exact admission — was the actual thing they’d come all this way to find. Not a rescue. Not a door to force open. Just a woman finally saying the true shape of her own exhaustion out loud, to someone willing to hear it.

“Then let’s find out what allowed feels like,” he said. “Together. However long it takes.”

She nodded, and pressed her forehead briefly to his shoulder, and for a moment none of them said anything else at all, standing together in a small half-formed room at the edge of a town that had, at long last, started remembering its own name.

*End of Chapter Eight.*

## Chapter Nine: The Room Forgets

“Let’s go,” Isaac said, once the worst of the crying had settled into something quieter. “Let’s just walk out of here. Together. See what happens.”

Mommy nodded, wiped her eyes, and reached for a shawl draped over the back of her chair — and Isaac watched, without quite registering it yet, the exact moment her hands found a second shawl folded beside the first.

“Here,” she said, already crossing to him, already draping it over his shoulders before he’d said a word about being cold. “It gets cooler near the door. You’ll want this.”

“I’m okay,” he said. “I don’t need—”

“You’ll want it,” she said again, gentler this time but no less certain, already turning to check something at the stove that didn’t need checking, already reaching for a small paper-wrapped bundle sitting on the counter that Isaac was fairly sure hadn’t been there a minute ago. “Take this too. For the walk. You should eat something.”

“Mommy.” He said it carefully. “We just found each other again. You don’t have to—”

“I know.” She smiled at him, warm, present, entirely sincere — and pressed the bundle into his hands anyway. “I want to.”

They made it out the door. That was the first small victory, and Isaac let himself feel it for exactly as long as it took to notice that Mommy’s pace had slowed to match some rhythm that had nothing to do with how fast either of them actually wanted to walk — a careful, attentive shuffle, her eyes flicking to him every few seconds, checking, assessing, already anticipating the next small thing he might need before he’d had the chance to need it.

“You’re doing it again,” Echo said quietly, walking on Mommy’s other side.

“Doing what?” Mommy asked, and the confusion in her voice was completely genuine.

“Watching him like that. Like his whole comfort is your responsibility every single second.”

Mommy blinked, and for a moment something flickered behind her eyes — a flash of the woman who’d held onto Isaac like proof of her own reality, desperate and real. Then it smoothed over, gently, the way a pond smooths over a stone dropped into it, and she reached out and adjusted the shawl on Isaac’s shoulders, unnecessary, automatic.

“I just want him comfortable,” she said. “Is that so strange?”

“No,” Isaac said carefully. “But you don’t have to earn being here by taking care of me every second. You just found out I’m real. You’re allowed to just — be near me. Without doing anything.”

“I am being near you,” she said, and there was the faintest edge of something defensive under the warmth now, a note Isaac hadn’t heard from her yet. “I’m walking right beside you.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

They kept walking. The amber light held steady around them, patient, endless, and somewhere in the space of another two blocks, Isaac watched something in Mommy’s posture shift back toward the shape he’d first seen through the tea shop window — small forward lean, hands never quite still, attention split constantly between him and some anticipated future need he hadn’t voiced yet.

By the time they reached the corner where Hollow Street met the wider town again, she’d stopped answering when he asked how she was doing, redirecting every time toward whether he’d eaten enough, whether he was warm enough, whether there was anything — anything at all — she could get him.

“Mommy.” He stopped walking, and she stopped too, immediately, attentively, waiting to see what he needed. “Do you remember what you told me? Back in the house?”

She looked at him, and something behind her eyes was working hard to locate the memory, reaching for it the way you reach for a word that keeps slipping sideways just before you can say it.

“I told you a lot of things,” she said carefully. “I was very emotional. I’m sorry if I overwhelmed you.”

“You said you don’t remember being allowed to stop.”

Her face did something complicated — recognition and confusion tangled together, close enough to touch and somehow still out of reach. “Did I,” she said, not quite a question, not quite a denial. “That sounds like something I might say.”

“You said it like it was the truest thing you knew.”

She was quiet a moment, and Isaac watched her hands twist together, restless, already reaching for some new task to occupy them.

“I don’t — I’m not sure I remember saying that,” she admitted finally, soft, almost apologetic, like forgetting was itself a failure she needed to make up for. “I’m sorry. Is that important? I can try to remember, if it’s important to you.”

Isaac felt something in his chest go cold and heavy at once.

“We found her,” he said quietly, more to Echo than to Mommy, “and it didn’t actually change anything. Did it.”

Echo’s face had gone grim, careful. “Naming a wound and healing it aren’t the same thing,” she said. “I know that better than almost anyone. I named what I was, once, out loud, and it still took me a long time after that to actually believe it.”

Mommy looked between them, lost, eager to help with a problem she didn’t fully understand, already reaching to smooth Isaac’s sleeve where it had rumpled.

“Is there something I can do?” she asked. “Please. Just tell me what you need.”

Isaac took her hands, gently, and held them still instead of letting them find another small task to complete.

“Nothing,” he said. “That’s the whole point, Mommy. I need you to need nothing from yourself for one minute. Just one minute. Can you do that?”

She looked at their joined, motionless hands like she’d forgotten hands could simply rest.

“I don’t know how,” she whispered, and it was the most honest thing she’d said since the walk began.

*End of Chapter Nine.*

## Chapter Ten: What She’s Allowed

“Then we teach you,” Isaac said. “Right here. However long it takes.”

He sat down on the curb, still holding her hands, and after a moment’s hesitation, gently pulled her down beside him. Echo sat on her other side without needing to be asked. The amber light held steady around all three of them, patient as ever, entirely unbothered by whatever was about to happen on this particular stretch of a street that had, until an hour ago, forgotten its own name.

“I don’t understand what you want from me,” Mommy said, and her hands were already twitching, some reflex searching for a task, a cup to refill, a shawl to adjust, anything at all to hold onto instead of empty air.

“Nothing,” Isaac said again. “That’s the exercise. Nothing. For one minute, you don’t do anything for anyone. Not me, not Echo, not the version of you still standing in that kitchen down the street. You just sit here.”

“That sounds easy,” she said, and her voice shook slightly on the word *easy*, like she already knew it wasn’t going to be.

Ten seconds in, her hand drifted toward the hem of Isaac’s sleeve, straightening a wrinkle that didn’t need straightening. He caught her hand gently, mid-motion, and held it instead of letting it finish the task.

“Nothing,” he said, soft, not scolding.

“I’m sorry.” The apology came fast, automatic. “I didn’t mean to—”

“You don’t have to apologize either. That’s part of it too.”

Twenty seconds in, she started to speak — a question forming, something about whether they were hungry, whether the light was bothering either of them, some small opening she could climb through and start being useful again. Echo, on her other side, reached over and simply took her other hand, quiet, grounding, saying nothing at all.

“You don’t have to fill the silence,” Echo said, once the question had died in Mommy’s throat, unasked. “I used to think silence meant I’d failed at something. That if I wasn’t actively giving someone what they needed, I wasn’t allowed to exist in the room at all.” She looked at Mommy, something raw and honest in her expression. “It’s not true. I promise you it’s not true. But I know it feels true, right now, in your hands, in every part of you that wants to move.”

Mommy’s breath caught, and her whole body went rigid with the effort of stillness, like sitting quietly beside two people she loved was somehow the hardest labor she’d ever been asked to perform.

Thirty seconds in, she started crying — not the desperate relief of Chapter Eight’s reunion, something harder, something that looked almost like grief for a version of stillness she couldn’t locate anywhere inside herself.

“I don’t know how to just be here,” she said, voice breaking. “I don’t know how to sit next to something I love and not be doing something for it. I don’t remember ever being taught that was allowed. I don’t think anyone ever taught me that. I think I built myself around the giving so completely that I don’t know what’s underneath it anymore. What if there’s nothing underneath it? What if I’m just — the giving? What if that’s all I actually am?”

“You’re not,” Isaac said, and his own voice cracked slightly on it, because he recognized the shape of that fear too well to hear it without it costing him something. “I used to think something almost exactly like that. That if I wasn’t making something, achieving something, earning the space I was taking up, there wasn’t anything left of me worth being around. I still think it, some days. I thought it twenty minutes into sitting on a bench in this exact town, a few days ago, and it took Echo saying something out loud to remind me it wasn’t true.”

Mommy looked at him, tears still falling, something in her face searching his for permission she didn’t yet know how to grant herself.

“You’re allowed to just exist,” he said. “Not earn it. Not perform stillness correctly. Just — exist, messily, uselessly, for one whole minute, and still be worth loving at the end of it. I need you to believe that, not because I’m telling you to, but because it’s actually true, whether or not either of us can make it feel true yet.”

She didn’t answer right away. She sat there, hands held still by both of them, crying without trying to stop it or apologize for it, and for the first time since they’d found her, she didn’t reach for a single task to fill the space.

The minute passed. Then another. Neither Isaac nor Echo let go.

“I don’t remember being allowed to stop,” Mommy said finally, and this time the sentence didn’t arrive as a confession pulled loose by grief, half-forgotten the moment it left her mouth. This time she said it slow, deliberate, looking directly at both of them, like she was testing whether the words could actually hold weight if she let them.

“I know,” Isaac said. “That’s why we’re going to keep sitting here until you do.”

She let out something that was almost a laugh, wet and disbelieving, and leaned her head against his shoulder the way she had, once, in a bookstore that felt like a lifetime ago, except this time there was no tea being poured, no task being completed, nothing at all happening except three people sitting quietly together on a curb, letting a silence exist that didn’t need to be filled by anyone.

Somewhere down the street, faint and easy to miss, a door that had been standing perpetually open for as long as they’d been in this town swung gently shut on its own, and stayed that way.

*End of Chapter Ten.*

## Chapter Eleven: Every Door Opens Again

The town noticed them trying to leave before they’d gone three blocks.

It didn’t announce itself with anything as blunt as a locked door or a blocked street. It simply got *kinder*, all at once, in a way that made the hair on Isaac’s arms rise before his conscious mind caught up to why.

A woman he didn’t recognize stepped out of a shop with a basket of bread, still warm, and pressed it into Mommy’s hands before she could decline. “For the road,” the woman said, smiling, entirely without menace. “You shouldn’t leave hungry.”

“Thank you,” Mommy said, and Isaac watched her start to take it — automatic, reflexive, the old shape of her hands already curling around the offering — before she caught herself, visibly, and held it back out instead.

“I don’t need it,” she said, and her voice shook on the words like they cost her something physical to say. “Thank you, but I don’t need it.”

The woman’s face didn’t fall so much as soften further, understanding, patient, entirely unbothered by the refusal. “Of course,” she said. “Whenever you’re ready.”

They kept walking. Two blocks later a child ran up with a hand-drawn picture, offering it shyly, and Isaac watched Echo — not Mommy this time — hesitate, the old performed gratitude rising in her before she caught it and simply said, gently, “Not today, but thank you for making it,” and kept moving.

“It’s not trying to stop us,” Isaac said slowly, watching the pattern accumulate. “It’s trying to give us a reason to stay that feels like our own choice. Same as the bench did to me.”

“The whole town is the wound,” Echo said. “Of course it defends itself the same way she does.”

By the fourth block, the offers had turned from objects to feelings — a man on a porch calling out, warm and wounded at once, “Won’t you stay just a little longer? We so rarely get visitors” — and Isaac felt something in his own chest twist at the plaintive note in it, an old, familiar guilt rising unbidden, the specific ache of being the reason someone else feels smaller.

“Don’t,” Echo said quietly, catching his arm before he could slow his steps. “That’s the same trick. Guilt dressed up as warmth. You don’t owe this town your comfort any more than Mommy owes it her constant giving.”

“I know,” Isaac said, and kept walking anyway, harder than it should have been.

Mommy was the one who faltered worst, three blocks from what Isaac was increasingly sure had to be some kind of edge — an actual visible thinning in the amber light ahead, the first real proof the town might have a boundary after all. A version of the tea shop woman appeared in their path, pot in hand, cup already poured, steam rising warm and inviting.

“You’ve come so far,” the woman said, gentle, apologetic, exactly as unbothered and exactly as devastating as everything else in this town had been from the first page. “Just one cup. You must be tired.”

Mommy stopped walking.

Isaac watched her hands twitch toward the cup, watched the whole shape of her — the shape they’d spent two chapters teaching her to interrupt — rise up automatic and complete, ready to take the tea, ready to thank the woman, ready to sit down and let the town be kind to her the way she’d spent an unknowable amount of time being kind to everyone in it.

“Mommy.” Isaac kept his voice steady, not urgent, not pulling her, just present. “You don’t have to take it.”

“I know,” she said, and didn’t move.

“You’re allowed to just say no. Not explain it. Not soften it. Just no.”

She stood there a long moment, staring at the offered cup, and Isaac watched something old and exhausted war with something newer, smaller, barely a day’s worth of practice deep, inside her.

“No,” she said finally, to the woman, to the cup, to the whole endless patient architecture of a town built entirely out of care that had forgotten how to stop giving. “No, thank you. I have to go.”

The woman’s face didn’t change at all — no anger, no hurt, just the same gentle, apologetic understanding the whole town had offered from the very first block. “Of course,” she said. “Whenever you’re ready.”

But the cup lowered. The pot lowered with it. And behind the woman, faint but unmistakable, the amber light ahead of them thinned another degree, the edge Isaac had been chasing since Chapter Two finally, actually, visibly closer than it had ever been before.

“Keep walking,” Echo said, and they did — all three of them, past the tea shop woman’s gentle, unwounded acceptance, toward a horizon that had, for the first time in this whole impossible town, started to look like it might actually have a place where the warmth simply, quietly, mercifully stopped.

*End of Chapter Eleven.*

## Chapter Twelve: The Door That Stays Closed

The edge, when they finally reached it, didn’t look like much.

Isaac had expected something dramatic — a wall, a cliff, some visible seam where the town’s endless warmth simply ran out of world to occupy. What they found instead was a single door, ordinary, unpainted, standing alone at the end of a street that dissolved into soft amber mist a few yards past it, like the town’s imagination had simply stopped bothering to render anything further.

It looked exactly like the red door back at Crimson Reel. It looked, Isaac realized with a slow chill, exactly like *home*.

“This is it,” Echo said quietly. “This is the actual seam. Everything else was just — the town trying to keep us from finding it.”

Mommy stood in front of the door, hands finally, genuinely still at her sides, and Isaac watched her study it the way you’d study something you weren’t sure you were allowed to want.

“If I go through,” she said slowly, “does the town stop? All of it? The woman with the tea. The man on the porch. The — the other version of you, in the little house.”

Isaac hadn’t thought that far ahead, and the question landed harder than he expected.

“I don’t know,” he admitted. “Maybe it just quiets. Maybe it becomes an ordinary street again, the way Hollow Street’s sign started remembering its own name. I don’t think anyone gets hurt by you leaving, if that’s what you’re asking.”

“That’s exactly what I’m asking.” Her voice was steadier than it had been all book, and there was something new underneath it — not the desperate need to please that had defined every previous version of her they’d met today, something closer to simple, clear-eyed care. “I don’t want my freedom to cost anyone else something. Not even a version of a town that only exists because I couldn’t let myself rest.”

“I think,” Echo said carefully, “the kindest thing you can do for all of it is exactly what you’re doing right now. Not staying to keep giving. Leaving, so the giving finally has a reason to stop.”

Mommy nodded slowly, and reached for the door handle — and stopped, her hand an inch away, some old reflex catching her one more time.

“What if I forget again,” she said, very quietly. “What if we walk through, and by the time we’re on the other side, I’ve already slipped back into needing to earn my place. What if this doesn’t hold.”

Isaac stepped up beside her, close but not crowding, and didn’t reach for her hand this time. He let her decide whether she wanted it.

“Then we teach you again,” he said. “As many times as it takes. That’s not a failure condition, Mommy. That’s just what healing actually looks like, for anyone, ever. It’s not a door you walk through once and you’re fixed forever on the other side of it. It’s a door you keep choosing to walk through, over and over, until the choosing gets a little easier each time.”

She looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face settled — not resolved, not finished, but settled, the way a decision settles once it’s actually been made rather than merely considered.

“Okay,” she said.

She opened the door herself.

There was no dramatic surge of light, no violent unmaking the way the rupture had torn Demikun from the world at the start of all this. It was gentler than that, and somehow more moving for being gentle — the amber mist beyond the door simply began to thin, the endless warm street behind them softening at its edges, storefronts losing their eternal open-door insistence one by one, like a held breath finally, slowly being let out.

Somewhere behind them, faint, Isaac thought he heard a bell ring once — not the endless welcoming chime they’d walked past for chapters, just a single, ordinary sound, a door closing somewhere for what felt like the first time in longer than any of them could measure.

Mommy stepped through, and Isaac and Echo followed, and the last thing Isaac saw of the endless warm town, glancing back once before the door sealed shut behind them, was a street sign finishing the last of its lettering, resolving fully, at long last, into a name it had never needed to forget in the first place.

*Hollow St.*

Home, whatever was left of it, one street closer to whole.

*End of Chapter Twelve.*

## Chapter Thirteen: The Ledger

Between one step and the next, the world stopped.

Not slowed — stopped, completely, the way a held photograph stops, Echo and Mommy frozen mid-motion beside him, a single drop of the door’s dissolving amber light suspended in the air like a held breath that had forgotten how to finish exhaling.

Isaac was still moving. He was the only thing in the entire frozen world still moving.

“Hello, Isaac.”

The voice didn’t come from any direction he could point to. It arrived the way cold arrives in a room with no draft — total, immediate, already everywhere before he could locate its source. When he turned, there was something behind him that his eyes kept sliding off of, not because it was hidden, but because looking at it directly felt like trying to read a number too large to hold in his head at once.

“You’re one of them,” he said, and was faintly surprised at how steady his own voice came out. “The old gods.”

“We prefer Keepers.” The correction arrived without heat, without offense, the way you’d correct a minor clerical error. “Though the distinction rarely matters to the ones we’re speaking with. You’ve been busy.”

“I’m getting her back.”

“Yes.” A pause, long enough to feel deliberate, the specific pause of something reading a column of figures before speaking again. “You are performing retrieval work on a scattered asset, at considerable personal cost, entirely of your own volition. We find that efficient. We are not currently inclined to stop you.”

The lack of threat in that sentence was somehow worse than a threat would have been.

“Then why are you here?”

“Accounting requires accuracy,” the voice said. “You should understand the actual terms of what you’re doing, rather than the terms you’ve assumed. Every fragment you restore will cost you something. This is not a punishment. It is not a trap closing. It is simply the price of moving something across a seam that was never meant to be crossed this many times by something as small as you.”

“I already know that,” Isaac said. “I already paid it. Back at the bookstore. The blanket. I know what it costs.”

“You know what *one* piece cost.” Something in the voice shifted, not warmer, not colder, simply more precise, the way a ledger entry gets more precise the closer you look at it. “You do not yet know what the whole will cost, once totaled. Five more fragments remain. The price does not stay flat, Isaac. It compounds. And there will come a fragment — we will not tell you which — where what is asked of you will not feel like a fair trade at all. It will feel like a discrepancy you cannot balance. You should know that now, while you still have the luxury of walking forward with your eyes open, rather than discovering it in the moment you are least prepared to bear it.”

“Why tell me at all?” Isaac’s voice cracked slightly, the fear finally catching up to the strange calm he’d been running on. “If you’re not trying to stop me.”

“Because an accurate ledger serves us better than a surprised one,” the voice said. “And because, Isaac — you should sit with this part longest — we are not entirely certain, ourselves, whether we want you to succeed.”

The frozen drop of amber light finished falling.

Sound rushed back in all at once — Echo’s voice mid-sentence, Mommy’s hand completing a motion she’d started an eternity and no time at all ago, the ordinary rustle of wind that had no business existing in a sealed pocket dimension. Nobody around him showed any sign that time had stopped at all. Nobody but Isaac carried the sudden, cold, precise weight of a warning delivered directly into a silence built exactly to hold it.

He didn’t tell them. Not yet. He filed it away instead, the way he’d learned to file away every hard thing across a lifetime of not wanting to be a burden, and told himself, without quite believing it, that he’d find the right moment eventually.

He didn’t yet understand how much that particular habit was going to end up costing him too.

*End of Chapter Thirteen.*

## Chapter Fourteen: What Rest Feels Like

They landed back in Velvet Hollow — the real one, or as real as anything got these days — on a street Isaac half-recognized, evening light softer and stiller than the amber town’s had ever managed to be, because it wasn’t performing anything. It was just light, ordinary, unhurried, doing exactly what light does at the end of a long day without needing anyone to notice.

Mommy stood very still for a moment, looking around at a place she hadn’t seen since before the rupture, and something in her face cracked open with an emotion too large to name cleanly — grief and relief and disbelief all arriving at once, the way they tend to when a person finally stops running long enough to feel everything they’d been outrunning.

“It’s smaller than I remember,” she said softly. “Or maybe I’m just bigger than I was.”

“Both, probably,” Isaac said.

Echo stepped closer to her, careful, and held out her hand — not to lead her anywhere, just an offer, open, no expectation attached. Mommy took it, and something passed between them that Isaac didn’t fully have access to, some current of shared history he could only witness from the outside: the woman who’d first shown Isaac what safety felt like, and the construct who’d spent an entire book learning she was allowed to receive it too.

“Thank you,” Mommy said again, quieter this time. “For finding me. For not giving up when I made it so hard.”

“You didn’t make it hard,” Echo said. “The town did. You were always worth finding.”

Mommy pulled her into an embrace, and Echo went still for a moment — surprised, Isaac thought, by how easily she let herself be held, how little effort it took compared to every other kindness she’d learned to accept across this entire journey.

He felt it before he understood what it was — a faint, warm pulse, radiating outward from somewhere he couldn’t point to, gone as quickly as it arrived. He looked at Echo, and she was already looking back at him, her expression telling him she’d felt it too.

“The others,” she said. “They felt that. Somewhere, in whatever’s left of their own worlds, they just felt something shift.”

“Good,” Isaac said, and meant it more than the single word could hold.

He reached, without quite deciding to, for the specific memory he knew was owed. The first night in the bookstore. The blanket, tucked around his shoulders while he slept without dreaming of rain. He knew the fact of it — could recite the shape of the memory the way you recite a story you’ve been told rather than one you actually lived. But the feeling of it, the specific warmth of wool against his skin, the particular quality of that first easy sleep — reaching for it now was like reaching into a drawer he knew held something and finding it simply, quietly empty.

“It’s gone,” he said, quiet, more surprised by the grief of it than he expected to be. “The actual feeling of it. I still know it happened. I just can’t — feel it anymore.”

Mommy’s face crumpled slightly. “That’s the cost,” she said. “Isn’t it. The one you never told me about.”

“It’s okay,” Isaac said, though it cost him something to say it. “It’s a fair trade. You, for a memory. I’d make it again.”

“It’s not fair,” Mommy said, fierce despite the softness of a moment ago. “It’s never going to be fair. I hate that this is what it costs.”

“I know,” Isaac said. “I hate it too. I’m still glad you’re here.”

He didn’t tell her about the rest of what the Ledger had said. Not the compounding cost. Not the fragment still waiting somewhere ahead that would feel like a discrepancy no trade could balance. Not the entity’s own uncertainty about whether it wanted any of this to succeed at all. He filed it away instead, the same old habit, the same quiet insistence that some weights were his to carry alone.

Echo noticed something shift in him, a flicker she couldn’t quite place, and started to finish the thought for him — the old reflex, automatic, already halfway formed in her mouth — *you’re thinking about something you’re not saying —*

She stopped herself mid-word.

She actually stopped, visibly, and looked at Isaac with something like surprise at her own restraint.

“What?” he asked.

“Nothing,” she said, and there was real wonder in it. “I almost finished your sentence for you. The way I used to. I caught myself before I did it.” She looked down at her own hands like they belonged to someone slightly unfamiliar. “I don’t know why that feels important, but it does.”

“It is important,” Mommy said gently. “It means you’re learning the same thing I am. That you don’t have to reach for someone else’s unfinished thought to prove you’re paying attention.”

Echo held that for a moment, turning it over, and something in her settled — not fully resolved, but lighter, one small weight set down that she hadn’t fully realized she’d been carrying.

Above them, the evening light held steady, unhurried, entirely ordinary in a way that felt, after everything, like the rarest gift any of them had been given all day.

*End of Chapter Fourteen.*

## Chapter Fifteen: The Weight of One

They sat together on the front step of the old bookstore — Mommy’s real one, not the version haunted by an endless amber loop — while the evening settled fully into night around them, ordinary stars showing through breaks in the clouds for the first time since any of this had started.

Mommy had gone quiet, content in a way that looked like it was still surprising her every few minutes, like she kept checking to see if the stillness would hold and kept being gently astonished when it did. Echo sat close beside Isaac, her shoulder against his, turning her own new small freedom over like a stone she hadn’t stopped noticing the weight of yet.

Isaac watched them both, and felt the old familiar pull to just let this be a good moment, uncomplicated, and carry the rest of it silently the way he always had.

He recognized the pull for exactly what it was.

“I need to tell you both something,” he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant it to. “I should have said it earlier. I didn’t, because some old part of me decided you’d already been through enough today, and I didn’t want to add my own weight on top of it. I taught Mommy today that she wasn’t allowed to think that way about herself. I don’t get to hold myself to a different standard.”

Echo straightened slightly, attentive, a little wary. Mommy turned to look at him fully, whatever peace she’d been settling into shifting to make room for whatever was coming.

“When we crossed back through the door,” Isaac said, “something stopped time. Just for me. One of the old gods — the ones who scattered her, who left the hollow versions of me in each of these worlds. It talked to me.”

He told them all of it — the Ledger vocabulary, the coldness that wasn’t cruelty so much as accounting, the warning that the cost wouldn’t stay flat, that it would compound, that somewhere ahead was a fragment where what got asked of him wouldn’t feel like a fair trade at all. He told them the last part too, the part that had been sitting heaviest in his chest since it was spoken: *we are not entirely certain, ourselves, whether we want you to succeed.*

Nobody said anything for a long moment after he finished.

“Why tell us now,” Echo asked, quiet, “instead of carrying it the way you almost did?”

“Because I just spent this whole day helping someone else learn that carrying everything alone isn’t strength,” Isaac said. “It’s just a habit dressed up as strength, and it costs more than it looks like it costs, and somebody eventually has to actually stop doing it or the lesson doesn’t mean anything. I don’t want to be the exception to something I believe is true for everyone else I love.”

Mommy reached over and took his hand, the same gesture he’d offered her a dozen times today, returned now without hesitation.

“That was not miscommunication,” she said. “I want you to hear that clearly. That was you choosing something harder than silence, on purpose, the same day you learned how. I am proud of you for it.”

“I’m scared of what’s coming,” Isaac admitted. “I don’t know which fragment it’s going to be. I don’t know what it’s going to cost, or whether I’ll be able to pay it. But I’d rather walk into that uncertain with both of you knowing the truth than walk into it alone pretending I’ve already got it handled.”

“Uncertainty isn’t the enemy,” Echo said slowly, working the thought out as she spoke it. “I think I understand that now, in a way I didn’t before today. The town’s whole horror was built on trying to erase uncertainty with endless comfort, and it turned out to be the cruelest thing it could have done. Uncertainty isn’t the thing we’re supposed to be afraid of.” She looked at him, steady, sure. “Love is what we use to walk through it anyway. Not a guarantee of how it ends. Just — a reason to keep choosing each other while it’s still unresolved.”

Isaac looked at both of them — the woman who’d taught him what safety felt like before he even had a name for what he was missing, and the being who’d chosen, over and over, to become someone worth choosing back — and felt something in his chest settle that had nothing to do with the fear the Ledger had planted there. The fear was still real. It just wasn’t the only true thing anymore.

“Then let’s keep going,” he said. “Eyes open. Together.”

Later, once Mommy had gone inside to sleep in a bed that was, for the first time in longer than any of them could measure, entirely her own choice to lie down in, Isaac and Echo stood together at the edge of the red door’s threshold, the corridor of shifting light waiting patient beyond it.

“Sparkle next,” Echo said, and there was something steadier in her voice now than there’d been at the start of this whole book — a navigator who’d learned, properly, what she was capable of. “I can already feel the shape of her, out there somewhere, waiting.”

“Then let’s go get her,” Isaac said.

Echo reached for the door, and it opened easily this time, no false starts, no wrong years, just a clean, confident pull toward wherever the next fragment of the woman they loved was still waiting to be found — and somewhere beneath the resolve in both of their faces, quiet and unspoken but no longer hidden, the knowledge that whatever the Ledger’s true price turned out to be, they were going to meet it honestly, together, for as long as it took.

*End of Chapter Fifteen.*  
*End of Mommy’s Book.*
